# Building an early-career development plan

Choose one or two target job families and compare multiple real postings to identify recurring requirements. Inventory current skills with concrete evidence from classes, projects, work, and volunteering. Select a small number of priority gaps and set achievable practice goals with dates and deliverables. Revisit the plan as interests and market requirements change. Avoid treating one job description as a universal standard.

---

# Finding internships and gaining experience

Look for internships, campus employment, research assistance, volunteer work, student organizations, and course projects that offer meaningful responsibilities. Define a specific contribution and document what was learned. Ask supervisors or teammates for feedback. Evaluate opportunities for mentorship, safety, fair terms, and schedule fit. Do not present volunteer or class work as paid employment.

---

# Creating a credible project portfolio

Choose projects that demonstrate relevant tasks rather than collecting many unfinished examples. Each project should describe the question, data source, methodology, tools, individual contribution, results, and limitations. Include screenshots or demos where appropriate and make instructions reproducible. Use public, licensed, or synthetic data; remove private information and secrets. Explain how the project would be improved with more time.

---

# Networking and informational interviews

Build professional relationships through faculty, alumni, career events, student groups, and colleagues. Request short informational conversations with a specific purpose and respect the other person’s time. Ask about typical tasks, useful skills, entry paths, and advice for beginners rather than demanding referrals. Thank contacts and maintain relationships thoughtfully. Never imply a person endorsed an application without permission.

---

# Learning and feedback loops

After projects, interviews, and applications, reflect on what worked and what to improve. Ask for specific feedback when appropriate, but recognize employers may not provide it. Practice explaining work to nontechnical audiences. Track progress through demonstrated competencies, completed projects, and clearer communication rather than only application counts. Adapt plans to available time and resources.
